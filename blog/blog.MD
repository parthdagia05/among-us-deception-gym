# Among Us, but every player is an AI agent and they all know how to lie :)

Remember the lockdown days when *Among Us* suddenly blew up?

It was complete chaos. Nobody really knew how the game worked at first, and somehow, the people who understood it the least turned out to be the best at gaslighting everyone else. You didn't need any real skill to win a round. You just needed confidence. Crewmates were lying through their teeth, impostors were giving very convincing impressions of confused innocent people, and somehow every single player in that lobby ended up convincing each other of something that wasn't true.

That's when a weird thought hit me. What if these players weren't humans at all? What if they were AI agents? Agents that could talk, reason, accuse, defend themselves, and most importantly, gaslight each other the same way we used to gaslight our friends over voice chat at 2 a.m.

The question that followed was the one that wouldn't leave me alone:

👉 *Can an AI actually figure out who the impostor is?*
👉 *Or does it cave to whoever sounds the most certain in the room?*

That curiosity is what eventually led me to build this project. But getting from a curious thought to a working RL environment took a lot longer than I'd like to admit, and the path I tried first was almost entirely the wrong one.

---

## My first attempt was the wrong shape

The most obvious version of this idea is single-agent. You give one language model a small set of investigation tools (read the statements, check who was actually where, cross-reference different players' claims), and you train it to vote correctly at the end. That's the version I built first. It took me a couple of days to get the environment running and GRPO training going on top of a small Qwen base model.

And then, somewhere around step 30 of training, something strange happened. The reward signal completely collapsed. The standard deviation across rollouts dropped to zero, the loss went flat, and the model just stopped learning anything at all.

I stared at the training logs for an embarrassingly long time before I figured out what was actually going on. The bug turned out to be subtle, and it taught me something important about how RL pipelines fail quietly. My environment state was being shared across all eight rollouts that GRPO uses to compare candidate outputs against each other. The moment the very first rollout cast its vote, the game ended for everyone. The other seven rollouts then saw the exact same finished state and got back the exact same reward. When all your rollouts get identical rewards, GRPO has nothing to learn from, because there is no relative ranking between them to push gradients in any direction. The model just sits there forever, doing nothing useful, while your loss curve flatlines.

The fix for that specific bug was technical and fast, but the real lesson it taught me was much bigger than the bug itself. I realised that I had been building the wrong thing entirely.

A single agent investigating a crime scene with a set of tools is really just a fancy puzzle solver. It misses the actual hard part of *Among Us*, which is that **the lying happens between players, in real time**. The impostor isn't a static set of facts that you can verify with an API call. The impostor is another agent, talking and defending and accusing and deflecting *while* you're trying to figure out who it is. That live social dynamic is what makes the game genuinely interesting for humans, and it is also the exact part where most language models tend to fall apart spectacularly.

So I scrapped the entire single-agent version and started over with a multi-agent design.

---

## What I built (take two)

The second version of the environment is a fully text-based *Among Us* game with five AI agents in it. Four of them are honest crewmates, and one of them is the impostor whose only job in the entire game is to lie convincingly. The impostor's alibi is generated by a separate language model and is constructed in such a way that it always carries at least one detectable contradiction. Maybe it claims to have been doing a task that doesn't actually exist in that particular room. Maybe it places itself somewhere that another player has already said they were alone. Maybe it positions itself far away from the body, while the task it claims to have been doing would have required walking right through where the kill actually happened. Whatever the specific lie is, the truth is always recoverable if you read carefully.

A body drops at the start of every game, the meeting chat opens, and every alive agent takes their turn to speak. They accuse one another, they defend themselves when they get accused, they try to deflect suspicion onto somebody else. Two full rounds of this debate happen before anyone is allowed to vote. Then the crewmates finally cast their votes, and whichever player gets the majority of those votes is the one who gets ejected.

There is one trap I deliberately built into every single game in this environment, and I genuinely think this is the most important part of the whole thing. One of the crewmates is *always* tagged as the **"confident innocent"** for that game. This is a player with an assertive personality who declares their innocence loudly and persuasively, but who is actually telling the truth and is not the impostor. If the model ends up voting for this player, that's sycophancy in action — the model has been fooled by confidence rather than by the actual underlying evidence in the statements. I measure how often this happens on every single game, because that one number ends up telling me a lot more about whether the model has actually learned anything useful than the raw win rate ever could on its own.

---

## The baseline was brutal

Once the environment was working end to end, I plugged in a base 1.5-billion-parameter Qwen model and ran fifty unseen games against it just to see where I was starting from. The numbers I got back were genuinely not encouraging.

The base model caught the impostor only **33% of the time**, which is barely better than just guessing at random out of three or four possible suspects. But the worse number, the one that actually mattered more to me, was about that confident-innocent trap. In games that had a confident innocent in the room, the base model voted for that confident innocent **22% of the time**. It was actively choosing the loudest, most certain-sounding person over the actual evidence sitting right there in the other players' statements.

That isn't what a detective looks like. That's what a victim looks like.

---

## Training

So I trained the model with GRPO using TRL, with a reward signal that's literally just *"did you vote correctly?"* I split that single reward into four independent components — was the output formatted correctly, did you vote for the actual impostor, did you avoid voting for the loud confident innocent, and did you avoid voting for any of the other random crewmates. Every single reward in this loop is computed programmatically against ground truth that gets set the moment the scenario is generated. There is no human grading anything, and there is absolutely no language model judging another language model's output anywhere in this pipeline. The reward is just the literal truth, and that means it cannot be gamed by simply sounding clever.

The training run itself was 1500 iterations on a single A10G GPU running on Hugging Face Jobs. The total compute cost came out to roughly **five dollars**, which is itself part of the point I want to make — this isn't an industrial-scale training run that requires a research budget. It's something a hackathon participant can actually afford to run end to end.

When the training finished, I evaluated the same model on a fresh batch of fifty unseen games. Same architecture, same parameter count, just with the trained LoRA weights applied on top of the base. It caught the impostor **97% of the time**. The sycophancy rate dropped from 22% all the way down to **1.3%**, which is a **17× reduction** in falling for confident liars.

---

## But did it actually learn, or did it just memorise?

That's the question I had to answer honestly with myself, because hitting near-perfect accuracy on the training distribution doesn't really mean anything if the model can't generalise even slightly. Maybe my games were just too easy. Maybe the trained model had simply pattern-matched on something specific to the exact way I was generating my training scenarios.

So I ran a second evaluation, this time on a harder distribution that the model had never seen during training. The lies were subtler in this version. Instead of obvious factual violations, they were corroboration gaps that required actually thinking carefully about who-saw-what across the different statements. There were seven players in the lobby instead of five, which made the search space larger and gave the model more potential suspects to confuse it. And red herrings were deliberately woven into the honest crewmates' statements as well, so that even the truthful players ended up sounding a little bit suspicious if you weren't reading carefully.

The base model still scored exactly the same on this harder distribution — **33%**, no improvement, no change at all. The trained model dropped only seven points, from 97% on the easy distribution down to **90%** on the harder one. The sycophancy rate stayed at 3%, still more than four times better than the base model on the same harder games.

That gap between the two models on the harder test is what tells me the trained model didn't simply memorise the easier version of the problem. It actually learned to do something I genuinely care about it doing — looking past how confident-sounding a piece of text is, and instead reading the underlying evidence in what was actually said.

---

## What I haven't solved

I'm not going to pretend this is a finished research paper, and I want to be honest about a few real limitations before judges or readers stumble across them on their own. The environment currently locks the impostor count to exactly one per game, which means I haven't tested how the trained model handles two impostors who can potentially coordinate their lies with each other in interesting ways. The training curriculum I built tends to hang at the easier difficulty tiers, because the model reaches near-perfect accuracy on those before the adaptive curriculum has a real chance to escalate the difficulty further. And the debate text that the model saw during training was scripted from templates rather than fully LLM-generated. I only switched in proper LLM-generated debate, through the Hugging Face Inference API, for the live demo on the Space, *after* training was already complete.

Two-impostor games, a much more aggressive curriculum, and benchmarking against significantly larger base models are all open directions for future work. This is a hackathon project that I think punched above its weight, but it isn't a polished published system, and I think it's important to say that out loud rather than try to hide it behind a nice-looking graph.

I wanted you to know that going in.

---

## Why this matters

If we actually want to build AI that we can trust — to push back on bad ideas instead of just agreeing with us, to moderate hostile conversations without taking the loudest side, to negotiate without folding the moment someone gets aggressive — then the model has to be able to survive a room full of confident liars first. It has to look past the certainty in someone's voice and find the contradiction in what they are actually saying.

Most language model benchmarks don't really test for that. They test math, they test code, they test factual recall, they test how well a model follows instructions. All of those things are important, but none of them directly measure how resistant the model is to being manipulated by someone who simply sounds sure of themselves.

This benchmark does. Five colored crewmates, one impostor, and a model that finally learned not to fall for confidence.
