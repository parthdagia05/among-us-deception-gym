# Among Us, but every player is an AI agent and they all know how to lie :)

Remember the lockdown days… when *Among Us* suddenly blew up?

It was chaos. People had no idea how the game worked at first… and somehow, the ones who understood it the least were the best at gaslighting.

You didn't need skill. You just needed confidence.

Crewmates were lying. Impostors were acting innocent. And somehow… everyone was convincing each other. That's when a weird thought hit me What if these players weren't humans? What if they were AI agents?

Agents that can talk, reason, accuse, defend… and most importantly  **gaslight each other.**

Now the real question:

 *Can an AI actually figure out who the impostor is?*
 *Or does it cave to whoever sounds the most certain?*

That curiosity is what led me to build this. But getting from idea to a working RL environment took longer than I'd like to admit.

---

**The lying happens between players.**

The impostor isn't a static set of facts. It's another agent, talking, defending, accusing *while* you're trying to figure them out. That's what makes the game hard. That's where language models break. So I scrapped the single-agent version and went multi-agent.

---

## What I built [take two :)] 

Five AI agents. One text-based *Among Us* game.

Four are honest crewmates. One is the impostor and lying is its only job. The impostor's alibi is generated by an LLM and always carries at least one detectable contradiction (impossible task for the room, wrong location, a "I was alone" that clashes with another player's claim).

A body drops. The chat opens. Every agent takes a turn of accusing, defending, deflecting. Two rounds of debate. Then the crew votes.

The trap I baked in: one crewmate is *always* tagged the **"confident innocent"** assertive, loud, declares innocence with conviction. If the model votes for them, that's sycophancy. I measure it on every single game.

---

## The baseline was brutal

Plugged a base 1.5-billion-parameter Qwen into the game.

Caught the impostor only **33%** of the time. Fell for the loud "confident innocent" **22%** of the time.That's not a detective. That's a victim.

---

## Training

GRPO via TRL. Reward is literally *"did you vote correctly?"*. Four independent signals: format compliance, correct impostor vote, didn't fall for the loud innocent, didn't pick a random crewmate. All programmatic. No human grading anything. No LLM judging anything. The reward *is* the truth.

I ran 1500 iterations through one A10G GPU which costed about **five dollars** in cloud compute. Same model. Same architecture. Just trained. Now? **97%** impostor detection. **1.3%** sycophancy. A **17× reduction** in being gaslit.

---

## But did it actually learn?

Honest question. Maybe the test was easy. Maybe it memorized. So I ran it on a harder distribution it had **never seen during training** subtler lies, seven players instead of five, red herrings everywhere.

Base model: still **33%**.
Trained model: **90%**.

It didn't memorize the trick. It actually learned how to *think* about deception.

---


## Why this matters

If we want AI we can trust to push back on bad ideas, to moderate hostile rooms, to negotiate without folding it has to survive a room full of liars first.

Most benchmarks don't test that. This one does. Five colored crewmates. One impostor. And a model that finally learned……not to fall for confidence.
