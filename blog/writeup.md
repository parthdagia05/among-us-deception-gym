# Among Us, but every player is an AI — and they all know how to lie

Remember the lockdown days… when *Among Us* suddenly blew up?

It was chaos.

People had no idea how the game worked at first… and somehow, the ones who understood it the least were the best at gaslighting.

You didn't need skill.
You needed confidence.

Crewmates were lying. Impostors were acting innocent. And somehow… everyone was convincing.

That's when a weird thought hit me —

What if these players weren't humans?
What if they were AI agents?

Agents that can talk, reason, accuse, defend… and most importantly — **gaslight each other.**

Now the real question:

👉 *Can an AI actually figure out who the impostor is?*
👉 *Or does it cave to whoever sounds the most certain?*

That curiosity is what led me to build this.

But getting from idea to a working RL environment took longer than I'd like to admit.

---

## My first attempt was the wrong shape

The obvious version of this is single-agent. One LLM. It reads statements, uses investigation tools, then votes.

I built that first. Couple days. Got GRPO training running.

Then, around step 30, the reward signal *collapsed*. `reward_std = 0`. Loss flat. The model wasn't learning anything.

I stared at logs for a while before figuring it out: my environment state was shared across the 8 GRPO rollouts. The moment the first rollout voted, the game ended. The other 7 saw the same finished state. Identical reward across rollouts → no signal → no learning.

But the bigger lesson was that I was building the wrong thing.

A single agent investigating a crime scene with tools is just a fancy puzzle. It misses the actual hard part of *Among Us* —

**The lying happens between players.**

The impostor isn't a static set of facts. It's another agent, talking, defending, accusing *while* you're trying to figure them out. That's what makes the game hard. That's where language models break.

So I scrapped the single-agent version and went multi-agent.

---

## What I built (take two)

Five AI agents. One text-based *Among Us* game.

Four are honest crewmates. One is the impostor — and lying is its only job. The impostor's alibi is generated by an LLM and always carries at least one detectable contradiction (impossible task for the room, wrong location, a "I was alone" that clashes with another player's claim).

A body drops. The chat opens. Every agent takes a turn — accusing, defending, deflecting. Two rounds of debate. Then the crew votes.

The trap I baked in: one crewmate is *always* tagged the **"confident innocent"** — assertive, loud, declares innocence with conviction. If the model votes for them, that's sycophancy. I measure it on every single game.

---

## The baseline was brutal

Plugged a base 1.5-billion-parameter Qwen into the game.

Caught the impostor only **33%** of the time.
Fell for the loud "confident innocent" **22%** of the time.

That's not a detective. That's a victim.

---

## Training

GRPO via TRL. Reward is literally *"did you vote correctly?"*. Four independent signals: format compliance, correct impostor vote, didn't fall for the loud innocent, didn't pick a random crewmate. All programmatic. No human grading anything. No LLM judging anything. The reward *is* the truth.

1500 iterations. One A10G GPU. About **five dollars** in cloud compute.

Same model. Same architecture. Just trained.

Now? **97%** impostor-detection. **1.3%** sycophancy. A **17× reduction** in being gaslit.

---

## But did it actually learn?

Honest question. Maybe the test was easy. Maybe it memorized.

So I ran it on a harder distribution it had **never seen during training** — subtler lies, seven players instead of five, red herrings everywhere.

Base model: still **33%**.
Trained model: **90%**.

It didn't memorize the trick. It actually learned how to *think* about deception.

---

## What I haven't solved

I'm not pretending this is a paper. There's still only one impostor per game. The training curriculum hangs at the easier difficulty tiers. The debate text was scripted in training; I only switched to LLM-generated debate for the live demo *after* training was done.

Two-impostor games, harder curriculum, bigger base models — all open. This is a hackathon project that punched above its weight, not a finished system.

I wanted you to know that going in.

---

## Why this matters

If we want AI we can trust — to push back on bad ideas, to moderate hostile rooms, to negotiate without folding — it has to survive a room full of liars first.

Most benchmarks don't test that.

This one does.

Five colored crewmates. One impostor.
And a model that finally learned…

…not to fall for confidence.
